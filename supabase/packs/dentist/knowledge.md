# Dental knowledge for patients

General information about common dental treatments, written for patients. Every patient is different: the dentist decides what is suitable after an examination. Durations are typical ranges, not promises. This document deliberately contains no prices and no clinic-specific details.

## 1. Check-ups and professional cleaning (scaling and polishing)

**What it is.** A check-up is an examination of the teeth, gums, tongue and mouth, sometimes with X-rays. Professional cleaning removes plaque and hardened tartar (calculus) that brushing cannot remove, then polishes the teeth to remove surface stains.

**What happens.** The dentist or hygienist uses an ultrasonic scaler (a vibrating tip with a water spray) and hand instruments to remove tartar above and just below the gumline, then polishes the teeth. If there is gum disease, a deeper cleaning called scaling and root planing may be needed; it cleans the root surfaces below the gums, is usually done with local anesthetic, and may be split across two or more visits.

**Typical duration.** A routine check-up and cleaning usually takes 30 to 60 minutes. Deep cleaning takes longer and may need several visits.

**Aftercare.** Mild sensitivity to cold or slightly tender gums for a few days is common. Gums may bleed a little during or after cleaning if they were inflamed; this usually improves as the gums heal with good brushing and flossing.

**How often.** Many people are advised to have a check-up and cleaning about every 6 months. Some need it more often (for example people with gum disease, diabetes, or heavy tartar build-up) and some less often. The dentist will recommend an interval.

**Common fears and questions.**
- *Does cleaning damage or loosen teeth?* No. Scaling removes tartar, not tooth enamel. Teeth can feel different afterwards because tartar that was filling the gaps has been removed. Teeth that feel loose usually had gum disease before the cleaning.
- *Does it hurt?* Most people feel only scraping, vibration and some sensitivity. Numbing gel or local anesthetic can be used for sensitive areas or deep cleaning.
- *Why do my gums bleed?* Bleeding gums are most often a sign of gum inflammation (gingivitis) caused by plaque. Professional cleaning and daily brushing and flossing usually reverse gingivitis.

## 2. Fillings

**What it is.** A filling repairs a tooth damaged by decay (a cavity), a small crack or wear. Common materials are tooth-colored composite resin, glass ionomer, and silver-colored amalgam. The dentist recommends a material based on the size and position of the cavity.

**What happens.** The area is usually numbed with local anesthetic. The decay is removed, the tooth is cleaned and shaped, and the filling material is placed. Composite fillings are hardened with a blue curing light, then shaped and polished so the bite feels normal.

**Typical duration.** About 20 to 60 minutes per tooth, depending on size and location.

**Aftercare.**
- Numbness usually lasts 2 to 4 hours. Avoid chewing on the numb side and avoid very hot drinks until feeling returns, to prevent biting the cheek or tongue or burning the mouth.
- Composite fillings are fully set when the visit ends. With amalgam fillings, patients are often advised to avoid chewing hard foods on that side for about 24 hours.
- Mild sensitivity to cold, sweet or biting pressure for a few days, sometimes a couple of weeks, is common and usually fades.
- If the bite feels high, or sensitivity gets worse instead of better, the filling may need a small adjustment, so the patient should contact the clinic.

**Common questions.**
- *Why fill a cavity that does not hurt?* Early decay is often painless. Treating it early keeps the filling small and helps avoid a root canal or extraction later.
- *How long do fillings last?* Many years with good care, but fillings can wear, chip or develop new decay at the edges, which is why regular check-ups matter.

## 3. Root canal treatment (endodontic treatment)

**What it is.** Inside each tooth is soft tissue called the pulp, which contains nerves and blood vessels. When the pulp becomes badly inflamed or infected (from deep decay, a crack or an injury), root canal treatment removes it, cleans and disinfects the canals inside the roots, and seals them. It saves the natural tooth instead of extracting it.

**Signs it might be needed** (only a dentist can confirm, usually with an examination and an X-ray): lingering pain with hot or cold, severe toothache, pain when biting, a swollen gum or pimple-like bump near a tooth, or a tooth that has darkened. Sometimes there are no symptoms at all.

**What happens.** The tooth is numbed with local anesthetic. The dentist makes a small opening, removes the pulp, cleans and shapes the canals, and fills them with a sealing material. A temporary filling is often placed between visits. Afterwards, back teeth in particular usually need a crown to protect them, because a root-canal-treated tooth can become brittle.

**Typical duration.** One to three visits, each usually 60 to 90 minutes.

**Aftercare.**
- The tooth and gum can feel tender for a few days, especially when biting.
- Avoid chewing hard foods on that tooth until the final filling or crown is placed, because the tooth can crack.
- If there is severe pain, swelling, or the temporary filling falls out, the patient should contact the clinic.

**Common fears and questions.**
- *Is a root canal very painful?* With modern local anesthetic, most patients say the treatment feels similar to having a filling. It is done to relieve the pain caused by the infected pulp, not to cause it.
- *Is it better to just remove the tooth?* Keeping the natural tooth is usually preferred when possible, because a missing tooth can let neighboring teeth shift and affect chewing. The dentist will explain the options for each case.
- *Will the tooth be "dead"?* The nerve is removed, but the tooth stays in place and keeps working, supported by the surrounding gum and bone.

## 4. Tooth extraction (including wisdom teeth)

**What it is.** Removing a tooth that cannot be saved, for example because of severe decay, advanced gum disease, a fracture, crowding before orthodontic treatment, or a wisdom tooth that is impacted (stuck) or causing repeated problems.

**What happens.** A simple extraction removes a visible tooth after numbing the area with local anesthetic; the patient feels pressure but should not feel sharp pain. A surgical extraction, often needed for impacted wisdom teeth, may involve a small cut in the gum, removing a little bone, or dividing the tooth into pieces, and sometimes stitches. Some clinics offer sedation for anxious patients or complex cases.

**Typical duration.** A simple extraction often takes 20 to 40 minutes; a surgical or wisdom tooth extraction may take longer.

**Aftercare.**
- Bite firmly on the gauze pad for 30 to 45 minutes to help a blood clot form. The clot protects the socket and is essential for healing.
- For the first 24 hours: do not rinse vigorously, spit forcefully, drink through a straw or smoke, because these can dislodge the clot and cause a painful "dry socket". Avoid hot drinks and alcohol and rest.
- A cold pack held on the outside of the cheek, in short intervals, can help reduce swelling during the first day.
- Eat soft, lukewarm foods and chew on the other side. Keep brushing the other teeth, gently.
- After 24 hours, gentle warm salt-water rinses after meals help keep the area clean.
- Swelling and stiffness are common and often peak around the second or third day, then improve. The gum usually heals in one to two weeks; the bone takes longer.
- Take only the medicines the dentist has prescribed or advised, exactly as instructed.
- **Contact the clinic** if bleeding does not slow down after pressure, if pain gets worse after two to four days (a possible sign of dry socket), or if there is fever, pus, a bad taste, or swelling that keeps increasing.

**Common questions.**
- *Should a missing tooth be replaced?* Often yes, especially for teeth used for chewing or visible teeth, to prevent neighboring teeth from drifting. Options include implants, bridges and dentures (see below).
- *Do all wisdom teeth need removal?* No. Wisdom teeth that are healthy, fully erupted and easy to clean can often stay. The dentist decides based on an examination and X-rays.

## 5. Braces and clear aligners (orthodontic treatment)

**What it is.** Orthodontic treatment straightens crowded, gapped or crooked teeth and corrects bite problems. Straighter teeth can be easier to clean and can improve the bite and the smile.

**Braces.** Brackets are bonded to the teeth and connected with a wire that is adjusted over time to move the teeth gradually. Types include metal braces, tooth-colored ceramic braces, and lingual braces placed behind the teeth.
- Fitting braces usually takes 1 to 2 hours. Adjustment visits are typically every 4 to 8 weeks.
- Overall treatment commonly takes about 12 to 24 months, sometimes longer for complex cases.
- Teeth often feel sore for a few days after fitting and after adjustments; soft foods help. Orthodontic wax can be placed over a bracket that rubs the cheek.
- Avoid hard, sticky and chewy foods (for example hard nuts, ice, sticky sweets, chewing gum) because they can break brackets or bend wires.
- Brush carefully around every bracket after meals and clean between teeth with floss threaders or small interdental brushes.

**Clear aligners.** A series of custom-made, removable, nearly invisible plastic trays that move the teeth step by step. They suit many mild to moderate cases; the dentist or orthodontist decides whether aligners or braces are better for each person.
- Aligners need to be worn about 20 to 22 hours a day and are usually changed to the next set every one to two weeks, as instructed.
- They are removed to eat and to drink anything other than plain water. Brush the teeth before putting them back to avoid trapping food against the teeth.
- A new set can feel tight for a day or two.

**After treatment.** A retainer is needed to keep teeth in their new position, because teeth tend to move back. Retainers can be removable or a thin wire fixed behind the teeth. The dentist will explain how long and how often to wear it.

**Common questions.**
- *Am I too old for braces?* Adults of any age with healthy teeth and gums can have orthodontic treatment.
- *When should a child be checked?* An orthodontic check around age 7 is often suggested, so problems with jaw growth or bite can be spotted early, even if treatment starts later.

## 6. Dental implants

**What it is.** An implant replaces the root of a missing tooth. A small post, usually made of titanium, is placed into the jawbone. Over time the bone bonds to the implant (osseointegration). A connector (abutment) and a crown, bridge or denture are then attached on top.

**Suitability.** Good candidates generally have healthy gums and enough bone to hold the implant. If bone is lacking, a bone graft may be needed first. Smoking, uncontrolled diabetes and some other health conditions can affect healing, so the dentist reviews medical history and usually takes X-rays or a 3D scan before planning.

**What happens.** The implant is placed under local anesthetic in a minor surgical procedure; placing a single implant often takes about 1 to 2 hours. The bone then needs time to heal around it, commonly about 3 to 6 months, before the final crown is fitted. A temporary tooth can often be worn in the meantime. In some cases the tooth can be attached sooner; the dentist decides this.

**Aftercare.** Some swelling, bruising and discomfort for a few days are common. Follow the dentist's instructions, eat soft foods at first, avoid smoking, and keep the area very clean. Long term, implants are cleaned like natural teeth, with brushing, cleaning between teeth and regular check-ups.

**Common questions.**
- *Does it hurt?* The area is numbed during the procedure. Many patients describe the discomfort afterwards as less than they expected, similar to an extraction.
- *How long do implants last?* With good oral hygiene and regular check-ups, implants can last for many years, often decades. The crown on top may need replacing at some point because of normal wear.

## 7. Crowns and bridges

**Crowns.** A crown (or "cap") covers the whole visible part of a tooth to restore its shape, strength and appearance. It is used for teeth that are badly decayed, cracked, heavily filled, worn down, or treated with a root canal, and on top of implants. Materials include all-ceramic (porcelain), zirconia, porcelain fused to metal, and metal alloys.
- Usually two visits: at the first, the tooth is numbed and shaped, an impression or digital scan is taken, and a temporary crown is fitted. At the second, often one to two weeks later, the permanent crown is cemented. Some clinics can make crowns in a single visit with in-house milling equipment.
- With a temporary crown, avoid sticky and hard foods on that side and slide floss out sideways rather than pulling it up.
- Crowns commonly last 10 to 15 years or longer with good care.

**Bridges.** A bridge replaces one or more missing teeth with an artificial tooth held in place by crowns on the neighboring teeth (or on implants). It is fixed and not removed at home. Cleaning under the false tooth every day is important, using floss threaders, super floss or interdental brushes.

**Common questions.**
- *Will a crown look natural?* Tooth-colored crowns are matched to the shade of the neighboring teeth.
- *Can a crowned tooth still get decay?* Yes, at the edge where the crown meets the tooth, so brushing, cleaning between teeth and check-ups remain important.

## 8. Teeth whitening

**What it is.** Whitening lightens the natural color of teeth using a bleaching gel. It works on natural tooth enamel only: it does not change the color of crowns, veneers, fillings or dentures.

**Options.**
- *In-clinic whitening:* a stronger gel is applied while the gums are protected; a session usually takes about 60 to 90 minutes and can give a noticeable change in one visit.
- *Dentist-supervised home whitening:* custom-made trays are worn with a milder gel, usually for a set time each day over about one to two weeks, as instructed.
- Products bought without dental supervision vary widely in strength and safety; it is best to get advice from a dentist first.

**Before whitening.** A check-up is recommended first, because cavities, gum disease or exposed roots should be treated before whitening, and the dentist can check whether the staining will respond. Whitening is generally not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding, and is usually not done for young teenagers.

**Aftercare.** Temporary sensitivity and mild gum irritation are common and usually settle within a few days. For the first 48 hours, avoiding strongly colored food and drink (tea, coffee, red wine, colas, turmeric-rich curries) and tobacco helps the result. Results typically last from several months to a few years, depending on diet and habits; touch-up treatments can refresh them.

**Common questions.**
- *Does whitening damage enamel?* When done as advised by a dentist, whitening is considered safe for enamel. Overusing products or using them without guidance can increase sensitivity and gum irritation.
- *How white will my teeth get?* It varies from person to person. Yellowish stains usually respond better than grey stains.

## 9. Dentures

**What they are.** Removable replacements for missing teeth. Complete (full) dentures replace all the teeth in a jaw; partial dentures replace some teeth and clip onto the remaining natural teeth. They are made of acrylic, sometimes with a metal framework. Implant-supported dentures snap onto implants for extra stability.

**What happens.** Making dentures usually takes several visits over a few weeks: impressions, bite records, a try-in to check fit and appearance, and the final fitting. "Immediate dentures" can be fitted on the day teeth are removed, but usually need adjustment or relining as the gums heal and shrink.

**Getting used to them.** It can take a few weeks to get used to speaking and eating with new dentures. Start with soft foods cut into small pieces and chew on both sides. Sore spots are common at first; the dentist can adjust the denture, so the patient should not try to adjust it themselves.

**Care.**
- Remove and rinse dentures after meals. Clean them every day with a soft denture brush and a cleaner meant for dentures; regular toothpaste can be too abrasive.
- Clean the gums, tongue and any natural teeth every day.
- Many dentists advise taking dentures out at night to let the gums rest. Store them in water or a denture-soaking solution so they do not dry out and warp. Never use hot water.
- Handle them over a folded towel or a basin of water so they do not break if dropped.
- Over the years the gums and bone change shape, so dentures may need relining or replacing. Regular check-ups are still important, including for checking the soft tissues of the mouth.

## 10. Children's dentistry

**First visit.** A child's first dental visit is usually recommended within six months of the first tooth appearing, or by the first birthday. Early visits help prevent problems and let children get comfortable with the dentist.

**Baby teeth matter.** Baby (milk) teeth help children chew, speak clearly and hold space for the adult teeth. Decay in baby teeth can cause pain, infection and problems with the adult teeth underneath, so they should be cared for and treated.

**Teething and tooth timeline.** The first baby teeth usually appear around 6 months of age, and most children have all 20 baby teeth by about age 3. Adult teeth start coming in around age 6, usually starting with the first molars behind the baby teeth. Teething can cause drooling, chewing on objects and fussiness; a clean, chilled (not frozen) teething ring can help. High fever or illness is not a normal part of teething and should be checked by a doctor.

**Brushing and toothpaste.**
- Start cleaning the mouth even before teeth appear, by wiping the gums with a soft clean cloth.
- From the first tooth, brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste: a smear about the size of a grain of rice for children under 3, and a pea-sized amount from about age 3 to 6.
- Parents should brush or supervise brushing until about age 7 or 8. Encourage children to spit out toothpaste rather than swallow it.

**Preventive treatments.** Dentists may apply fluoride varnish to strengthen enamel, and dental sealants (thin protective coatings) on the chewing surfaces of back teeth to help prevent decay.

**Habits.**
- Avoid putting a child to bed with a bottle of milk, formula or juice, which can cause "bottle" tooth decay. Limit sweets and sugary drinks, especially between meals.
- Thumb-sucking and pacifier use are common in young children and usually stop on their own. If they continue after the adult front teeth start to come in, ask the dentist for advice.
- A mouthguard is recommended for contact sports.

**Children's dental injuries.** If a baby tooth is knocked out, do not try to put it back, because this can damage the adult tooth developing underneath; contact the dentist. A knocked-out adult tooth is an emergency: see "When to see a dentist urgently" below.

**Helping nervous children.** Speak positively about the dentist, avoid using it as a threat, and avoid words like "hurt" or "pain". Many clinics use simple "tell, show, do" explanations so children know what to expect.

## 11. Daily oral hygiene

- Brush twice a day for about two minutes, including last thing at night, with a soft-bristled toothbrush and fluoride toothpaste. Brush all surfaces of every tooth and along the gumline with gentle, small movements; hard scrubbing can damage gums and enamel.
- After brushing, spit out the toothpaste but avoid rinsing with lots of water, so the fluoride keeps protecting the teeth.
- Clean between the teeth once a day with floss or interdental brushes. Brushing alone cannot reach these areas, where decay and gum disease often start.
- Replace the toothbrush (or electric brush head) about every three months, or sooner if the bristles are frayed or after an illness.
- Clean the tongue gently with the toothbrush or a tongue cleaner to help with fresh breath.
- Limit how often sugary foods and drinks are consumed. Frequency matters more than quantity, so keep sweets to mealtimes rather than snacking throughout the day. Drink water between meals.
- Avoid all tobacco, including smoking, chewing tobacco, gutka and paan with tobacco. Tobacco causes staining, gum disease, slow healing and oral cancer. Areca nut (supari) can also harm the mouth.
- Wear a mouthguard for contact sports. People who grind or clench their teeth at night can ask the dentist about a night guard.
- Have regular check-ups as advised by the dentist, even when nothing hurts. Many problems are painless in the early stages.

## 12. Other common questions

**Sensitive teeth.** Short, sharp sensitivity to cold, hot, sweet or sour things can come from worn enamel, gums that have pulled back and exposed the roots, brushing too hard, tooth grinding, a cavity or a cracked tooth. A toothpaste for sensitive teeth helps some people, but a check-up is important to find the cause. Sensitivity that lingers or gets worse should be checked soon.

**Bleeding or swollen gums.** These are most often signs of gum inflammation (gingivitis), caused by plaque along the gumline. Keep brushing gently and cleaning between the teeth, and book a check-up and cleaning. Untreated, gingivitis can progress to periodontitis, which can make teeth loose. Gum problems are more common in smokers, people with diabetes and during pregnancy.

**Bad breath.** Usually caused by bacteria on the tongue and between the teeth, gum disease, a dry mouth, or certain foods and tobacco. Good daily cleaning of teeth, gums and tongue, drinking water and regular check-ups help. Persistent bad breath should be checked by a dentist.

**Dental X-rays.** Dental X-rays help find problems that cannot be seen during an examination, such as decay between teeth, infections and bone loss. Modern digital dental X-rays use a very low dose of radiation, and the dentist takes them only when needed. Patients who are or may be pregnant should tell the dental team.

**Dental care during pregnancy.** Check-ups and cleanings are safe and important during pregnancy, because hormonal changes can make gums more prone to inflammation. Patients should tell the dentist they are pregnant; routine treatment is often planned for the second trimester, but dental pain or infection should not be ignored at any stage.

**Local anesthetic and numbness.** Local anesthetic numbs only the area being treated and usually wears off within 2 to 4 hours. Patients stay awake and may feel pressure or vibration but should not feel sharp pain; they can tell the dentist if they do, and more anesthetic can be given.

**Dental anxiety.** Feeling nervous about the dentist is very common. It helps to tell the dental team, who can explain each step, agree on a stop signal such as raising a hand, take breaks, and keep appointments short. Some clinics offer sedation options for very anxious patients. Bringing headphones and music can also help.

**Health conditions and medicines.** Patients should always tell the dentist about their medical conditions (for example diabetes, heart conditions, bleeding disorders), pregnancy, allergies and all medicines they take, especially blood thinners, so treatment can be planned safely. Patients should not stop or change any medicine before dental treatment unless their doctor tells them to.

**Chipped or broken tooth.** Keep any broken pieces, rinse the mouth gently with water, and contact the clinic. Small chips can often be smoothed or repaired with tooth-colored filling material; larger breaks may need a crown or other treatment. A broken tooth with pain or bleeding needs to be seen urgently.

**Lost filling or crown.** Contact the clinic for an appointment. Keep a lost crown safe and bring it along, as it can sometimes be re-cemented. Avoid chewing on that side until it has been checked.

## 13. When to see a dentist urgently

Contact the dental clinic **immediately** (the same day) for:
- Severe or throbbing toothache, or tooth pain that keeps the person awake or does not settle.
- Swelling of the gum, face, cheek or jaw.
- Bleeding from the mouth that has not stopped after about 20 to 30 minutes of firm pressure with clean gauze or cloth, including after an extraction.
- A knocked-out adult (permanent) tooth.
- A broken, cracked or displaced tooth, especially with pain or bleeding.
- Injury to the mouth, teeth or jaw.
- A gum boil, pus, or a bad taste with pain or swelling, which can be signs of infection (an abscess).
- Severe pain several days after an extraction.
- A lost filling or crown, or broken braces wire, that is causing pain or cutting the cheek.

Go to the nearest hospital emergency department or call emergency services **straight away** for:
- Facial swelling that is spreading towards the eye or down the neck.
- Swelling with difficulty breathing, swallowing or opening the mouth.
- Swelling or toothache together with high fever or feeling very unwell.
- Bleeding that cannot be controlled.
- A serious injury to the face or jaw, or any head injury.

**Knocked-out adult tooth: first aid.** Time matters; the best chance of saving the tooth is to get to a dentist within about 30 to 60 minutes.
1. Pick the tooth up by the crown (the white chewing part), not by the root.
2. If it is dirty, rinse it very briefly with milk or clean water. Do not scrub it and do not wrap it in tissue.
3. If possible, gently push it back into its socket and bite on a clean cloth to hold it in place.
4. If that is not possible, keep it in a small container of milk, or in the person's own saliva (inside the cheek, only for adults who will not swallow it). Do not store it in plain water for long.
5. Go to the dentist immediately.

**See a dentist soon (within a few days) for:** a mouth ulcer, white or red patch, or lump that has not healed within two weeks; teeth that feel loose (in adults); gums that bleed regularly; sensitivity that is getting worse; or a cracked tooth without pain. A non-healing ulcer or patch should always be checked, as dentists also screen for oral cancer.
